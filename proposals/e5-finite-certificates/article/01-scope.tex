\section{The incremental objective}
\label{sec:objective}

The most useful next contribution to Forge is not more orchestration. It is a
small number of new reasoning domains with a precise admission test, a concrete
algorithm, and a short route from search output to a Lean proof. This proposal
adds three such domains. Their common feature is that they replace unbounded
execution or unfolding by a finite closure computation.

\subsection{What the audit establishes}

The Forge snapshot reviewed here is
\code{674521027d968d59f7b83220ed52304a85cb55e2}. Its merged article, status ledger,
structural algorithms, and provenance appendix already cover a great deal:
demand-directed theorem use, structural induction and generalization, auxiliary
lemma synthesis, exact polynomial sums, state-machine conserved quantities,
polynomial equality multipliers, and nonlinear and Boolean certificate
workers~\cite{forge,forge-structural,forge-status,forge-provenance}. Those ideas
are prerequisites, not contributions of this article.

The important boundary is more specific. The implemented state-machine
certificate in the structural section checks
\[
 I(F(s))=I(s),
\]
whereas an invariant only needs its zero set to be preserved. The same section
correctly notes that simultaneously searching for unknown $I_j$ and unknown
$H_{ij}$ in
\[
 I_i(F(s))=\sum_j H_{ij}(s)I_j(s)
\]
is a bilinear problem. The present proposal supplies a concrete alternative:
generate the required invariant ideal from the target, then compute the
multipliers after the generating set has stabilized. This changes both the
search problem and the fragment for which a completeness argument is available.

The audit read the merged algorithm and status material, rather than claiming
a byte-for-byte review of all nine historical submissions. Some long connector
responses were truncated; the successive structural ranges and the provenance
inventory were used to resolve the relevant boundary. An unresponsive repository
search index was not used as evidence that a term was absent. The exact locations
and limitations are recorded in \code{docs/audit.md}.

\subsection{A shared question, three different closures}

Consider a state $s$, an update $F$, and an observation $q$. Instead of asking
``which invariant should I guess?'', ask:
\begin{quote}
What additional observations must vanish now to ensure that the desired
observation vanishes after every possible future update?
\end{quote}

For linear maps, observations are row vectors and closure is linear span. For
polynomial maps, observations are polynomials and closure is an ideal. For
symbolic sums, observations are shifted summands and closure is taken modulo
known identities and telescoping differences. The mathematical structures differ,
but each gives a disciplined way to manufacture the auxiliary facts needed by
an induction proof.

This is more useful than a collection of isolated examples. A single linear
worker covers equivalence of finite-state numeric folds, linear recurrence
implementations, and affine accumulator programs. A single ideal worker covers
coupled nonlinear recurrences and invariants whose values change but whose zero
sets do not. A single summation worker produces proofs of recurrences for whole
families of finite sums rather than evaluating isolated inputs.

\subsection{The notation and outcome contract}

All executed searches use exact rational arithmetic. A finite alphabet
$\Sigma$ indexes finitely many total updates. The execution of a word
$w=a_1\cdots a_t$ is
\[
 F_w(s)=F_{a_t}(\cdots F_{a_2}(F_{a_1}(s))\cdots),
 \qquad F_\epsilon(s)=s.
\]
This convention is important: backward multiplication of observations reverses
how a new symbol is attached to a recorded forward execution word.

A successful result is a certificate for the exact supplied problem. A negative
result is a concrete counterexample evaluated in that problem. Resource
exhaustion or failure of a bounded template gives \Unknown. In the summation
worker, success certifies a \emph{recurrence}, not automatically a closed form
or uniqueness of its solution. These distinctions determine what each result
is allowed to do; they are not merely labels in a report.

The article distinguishes mathematical theorems about idealized algorithms,
executed Python validation, uncompiled Lean source, and proposed integration.
In particular, the proof that an unbounded ideal algorithm terminates does not
turn an interrupted Gr\"obner-basis computation into a negative answer.

\subsection{Relationship to established work}

Observable closure belongs to the linear-algebraic theory of weighted-automaton
equivalence; Kiefer gives a compact treatment of the classical field-based
algorithms~\cite{kiefer}. Polynomial backward reasoning by ideals has direct
antecedents in the program-analysis work of Cachera, Jensen, Jobin, and
Kirchner~\cite{cachera,celtique}. Creative telescoping is an established symbolic
summation method, including modern algorithms that keep certificates
compact~\cite{brochet-salvy}. The specific contribution here is to turn these
ideas into deliberately small, independently replayable workers at Forge's
existing boundary, and to expose the semantic obligations their integration
actually needs.

No claim is made that the demonstrations would defeat every existing tactic or
library lemma. A one-line application of a previously proved theorem may beat
any search. The improvement sought is the ability to discover a finite
explanation when that theorem or invariant has not yet been supplied.
