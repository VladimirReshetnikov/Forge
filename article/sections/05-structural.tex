\section{Structural search: changing the statement that is being proved}
\label{sec:structural}

This is the capability that most clearly exceeds local saturation. A saturation
engine is very effective when useful ground terms and useful lemmas are already
present. A much wider class of goals requires \emph{making one of those things
exist}.

\subsection{Demand-directed theorem use}

Trigger-based instantiation works forward, from terms present in the equality
state. The documented E-matching machinery is a baseline, not something to
discard~\cite{grind-ematch}. \forge{} adds a complementary backward direction.

Maintain an index over the \emph{conclusions} of approved theorems --- initially
a discrimination tree keyed by the target's head symbol and normalised type
structure. An entry records the theorem constant, its universe and type
parameters, its conclusion head, a discrimination key, the shapes of its
premises, and a cheap estimate of elaboration cost. Retrieve a bounded candidate
list, instantiate universes, and unify the theorem conclusion with the target.
Successful unification creates premises as AND obligations and instantiation
choices as OR alternatives, with explicit dependencies so that the conclusion
cannot become available before its premises are proved.

For a theorem of shape $\forall x,\;P(x)\to Q(f(x))$ and a goal $Q(f(u))$,
backward application immediately proposes the obligation $P(u)$ and introduces
useful terms even when no suitable trigger was present in the initial forward
state. For a goal $Q(t)$ with arbitrary $t$ it does \emph{not} justify inventing
an inverse of $f$: the system must unify $t$ with an application already known
to be equal, synthesise a candidate $u$ and prove $f(u)=t$, or decline.

\begin{remark}
This is not a claim that E-matching could never reproduce the same argument with
different patterns and annotations. What a conclusion index removes is the need
for a human to have chosen those patterns in advance. Conclusion-selected
backward patterns already exist in \grind{} in some form~\cite{grind-paper}; the
proposed addition is that a retrieved theorem's unproved premises become
persistent obligations that other workers can be scheduled against.
\end{remark}

Premise retrieval should combine the target with currently \emph{blocked}
obligations, not just a bag of symbols from the original goal. A theorem
resolving a denominator guard can be more useful than one mentioning the final
function symbol. Start with local hypotheses, the equations of mentioned
definitions, relevant extensionality lemmas, and a small retrieved set; expand
only if those fail.

\subsubsection*{Solving for the missing term}

Backward matching does not determine every parameter, and the honest responses
to that are limited. In $\forall x\,y,\;R(x,y)\to P(x)$, a demand for $P(a)$
leaves $y$ unknown. \forge{} must not silently choose an arbitrary inhabitant.
It can enumerate appropriately typed terms from the local context, ask a witness
engine, exploit equalities, or leave the application suspended. Every selected
instantiation is checked by Lean, and typeclass synthesis and inhabitation
obligations are scheduled explicitly rather than assuming an arbitrary type has
an element.

A sharper technique applies when matching stalls at an \emph{interpreted}
subterm. Rather than failing, emit an equality constraint between that subterm
and the corresponding source expression, and solve for the missing binder.

\begin{lstlisting}
matchDemand(theorem, goal):
  freshen theorem parameters in a child metavariable context
  unify rigid heads and directly matching arguments
  collect the remaining argument equations
  dispatch recognized affine equations to witness synthesis
  enumerate small typed terms only for the still-free parameters
  apply the theorem with a complete candidate assignment
  retain every generated side condition as an obligation
  accept only after all obligations and the final proof replay
\end{lstlisting}

For hypotheses $\forall k\in\Z,\;P(2k+1)$ and $n\bmod 2=1$ with goal $P(n)$, the
controller synthesises $k=n/2$ from the arithmetic shape and proves
$2(n/2)+1=n$ using the oddness assumption, then transports along that checked
equality. For a conclusion containing $f(t+1)$ against a goal involving $f(s)$,
the candidate $t=s-1$ comes from solving $t+1=s$ over $\Z$ --- and over $\N$ the
same surface subtraction has different semantics and requires a guard.

Two disciplines make this safe. An arithmetic identity used to match arguments
must appear in the proof as rewriting or transport; it cannot be smuggled into
the unifier as an unverified definitional equality. And several triggers or
repeated binders produce a \emph{joint} constraint system: solving each
occurrence independently loses the equality relating two occurrences of the
same variable. Cache instances by theorem identity, universe and type
instantiation, the complete substitution, and the valid branch context. A proof
of $P(x)$ under $h:x=0$ is not a proof of the same displayed goal under
$h:x=1$.

\subsection{Demand slicing in a finite Horn fragment}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python by four independent efforts, in both
propositional and first-order ground variants.}

The prototypes isolate the mechanism in a fragment small enough to prove things
about: finite ground definite Horn programs. Backward relevance closure selects
the rules that could contribute to the target; forward chaining then runs only
on that slice.

\begin{proposition}[Slicing preserves derivability]
\label{prop:slicing}
For a finite ground definite Horn program, restricting forward chaining to the
backward conclusion-dependency closure of a target preserves whether that target
belongs to the least model.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A derivation of the target is a finite tree of rule applications. Starting from
its root, every rule used and every premise of every such rule is included by
the backward closure, so every derivation of the target is retained. Conversely
every rule of the restricted program belongs to the original program, so any
restricted derivation is valid in the original. Forward fixed-point evaluation
computes derivability in both finite programs. Cycles without a supporting fact
create no derivation in either.
\end{proof}

Termination and non-circularity are handled by \emph{tabling}, not by a
depth-first convention that treats ``currently being visited'' as success. Each
rule instance carries a counter of unresolved premises; input facts initialise
the agenda; proving a premise decrements the counters of its dependents; a rule
fires only when every premise is proved, and the proof node is stored at that
moment. Rules $P\to Q$ and $Q\to P$ with no base fact prove neither.

\begin{proposition}[Relative completeness of the finite core]
For a fixed finite set of ground candidate rule instances, if the dependency
agenda is exhausted without a resource cutoff, every fact in their least forward
closure is eventually proved. In particular, a backward-relevant closure
containing a ground derivation of the target will discover a proof.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Induct on the height of a finite derivation. Height-zero input facts enter the
agenda initially. Once all smaller-height premises of an instance have entered,
its unresolved counter reaches zero and its conclusion is enqueued. Tabling
ensures that repeated discoveries do not obstruct propagation.
\end{proof}

The certificate is a topologically ordered DAG: every premise index is strictly
smaller than its conclusion's index; the substitution domain must be exactly the
head's variables; and the head instantiation and premise alignment are checked.
A dependency cycle in the \emph{search} graph may be legitimate, but the final
proof certificate cannot justify itself through that cycle.

Two honesty constraints govern how this is measured, and
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} keeps four separate Horn experiments apart for
exactly this reason. First, the cost of \emph{building} the conclusion index
over all input rules is part of the demand-directed method's cost; none of the
prototypes obtains a sublinear end-to-end runtime merely by activating fewer
rules. Second, proof minimisation reduces even a full-closure derivation to the
same small proof, so \emph{proof size alone conceals the search-work
difference} --- the interesting quantity is rule firings, not the length of the
answer.

\begin{remark}
None of these Horn engines is an implementation or emulation of \grind{}'s
E-matching. They compare two deliberately small search policies against each
other, and one of them is a fair semi-naive forward-chaining baseline rather
than a straw man.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Induction: selecting a scheme and generalising the statement}
\status{The list and natural-number fragments, changing-argument detection,
bounded right-hand-side synthesis, structural induction, and trace replay are
implemented. General dependent induction and e-graph-guided frontier discovery
are specified only.}

The induction planner starts from the recursive applications visible in the
target and in relevant hypotheses. For each function it records which argument
positions are destructed by pattern matching, which are passed to recursive
calls as proper subterms, and which non-structural arguments change. Candidates
are ranked by how many blocked recursive calls they expose after one constructor
split. A variable is not selected merely because its type has an induction
principle, and blind induction on every natural variable is not acceptable.

A case split on an inductive value does not automatically supply an induction
hypothesis; confusing the two is a design error. Each candidate plan carries an
explicit Lean motive, an induction principle, the generalised variables, and the
recursive hypotheses available in each case. User-specified schemes take
priority.

Generalisation follows a \emph{dependency closure}, and this is where a naive
implementation goes wrong. In a context containing $x:A$, $y:B(x)$, and
$h:C(x,y)$, induction on $x$ requires a motive of the form
\[
  M(x)\;:=\;\forall y:B(x),\; C(x,y)\to P(x,y),
\]
not a proposition containing the old fixed $y$ at every new value of $x$. If a
generalised variable occurs in the type of another binder, that binder must be
generalised or explicitly transported as well. A textual substitution that
ignores dependent hypotheses is not a correct generalisation algorithm, and a
well-typed-looking first-order erasure is not a substitute for building the
motive through Lean's elaborator.

Generalisation is an \emph{OR alternative}, not an irreversible preprocessing
step: too much generality can make a useful theorem false or unnecessarily
difficult. Candidate generalisation sets are ordered by size and capped.

\subsubsection*{The accumulator example, including the obstruction}

Write $A$ for append, $R$ for reverse, and $\mathrm{RA}$ for accumulator
reverse:
\begin{align*}
A([],y)&=y,& A(h::t,y)&=h::A(t,y),\\
R([])&=[],& R(h::t)&=A(R(t),[h]),\\
\mathrm{RA}([],a)&=a,& \mathrm{RA}(h::t,a)&=\mathrm{RA}(t,h::a).
\end{align*}
The requested theorem is $\mathrm{RA}(x,[])=R(x)$. Inducting on $x$ without
generalisation gives an induction hypothesis about $\mathrm{RA}(t,[])$, whereas
the recursive call in the step case is $\mathrm{RA}(t,[h])$. \emph{Increasing a
rewriting budget cannot make this particular hypothesis say something
stronger.} This is the shape of obstruction the planner exists to detect.

The changing-argument detector observes that argument~0 is destructed
structurally while argument~1 changes from $a$ to $h::a$. It replaces the fixed
empty accumulator with a fresh parameter and asks for a right-hand side in a
typed grammar
\[
  L ::= []\;\mid\; x\;\mid\; a\;\mid\; R(L)\;\mid\; A(L,L),
\]
enumerated in increasing syntax-tree size. The successful candidate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:accspec}
\forall x\,a,\qquad \mathrm{RA}(x,a)=A(R(x),a).
\end{equation}
The base case is immediate. The step case uses the generalised hypothesis
$\mathrm{RA}(t,h::a)=A(R(t),h::a)$ against
\[
A(R(h::t),a)=A(A(R(t),[h]),a)=A(R(t),A([h],a))=A(R(t),h::a),
\]
the middle step being append associativity. Instantiating \eqref{eq:accspec} at
$a=[]$ and using append's right identity proves the original theorem.

A second family makes the same point over $\N$. For
\[
 A(0,a)=a,\qquad A(n+1,a)=A(n,a+2n+1),
\]
the theorem $A(n,a)=a+n^2$ needs $a$ generalised before induction; the step then
reduces to $a+2n+1+n^2=a+(n+1)^2$. The automation problem is choosing the
generalisation, not performing the final ring calculation.

\subsubsection*{A worked cross-engine plan: an accumulator \emph{inequality}}

The examples above are equations. The mechanism matters at least as much for
inequalities, where the induction planner and the arithmetic worker each supply
something the other cannot. Let
\[
\mathrm{energyAcc}([],a)=a,\qquad
\mathrm{energyAcc}(x::xs,a)=\mathrm{energyAcc}\bigl(xs,\;a+(x-1)^2\bigr),
\]
and take the goal
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
\forall xs\,a,\qquad a\le\mathrm{energyAcc}(xs,a).
\end{equation}
The planner identifies $xs$ as structural, detects that the accumulator changes
in the recursive call, and generalises $a$. The base case is reflexivity. In the
step, the generalised induction hypothesis gives
\[
 a+(x-1)^2\le\mathrm{energyAcc}\bigl(xs,\,a+(x-1)^2\bigr),
\]
leaving the demand $a\le a+(x-1)^2$. The ordered-algebra worker proves
$(x-1)^2\ge0$, the linear worker adds $a$, and transitivity closes the step.
\emph{Four} components contribute and none of them solves the problem alone.

If the update were an expanded polynomial such as $q(x,y)=x^2+6xy+9y^2$, the
same plan would call the exact quadratic decomposition of
\S\ref{sec:quadratic} to discover $q=(x+3y)^2$. If the update were nonnegative
only under a loop invariant, that invariant would have to be proved and threaded
through the induction motive --- an arithmetic certificate cannot supply an
invariant about recursive calls.

One of the source packages contains an ordinary Lean proof of \eqref{eq:energy}
as an uncompiled integration target. It is \emph{not} claimed that a planner
generated it; its purpose is to make the intended constructor sequence concrete
enough to validate once a pinned project exists.

\begin{remark}[Sampling rejects; it never proves]
Two prototypes filter grammar candidates against finite sample environments ---
one uses all 49 pairs of lists of length at most two over a two-element
alphabet. Samples \emph{only reject}. The surviving candidate is accepted solely
because it then passes symbolic induction and independent trace replay.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{What a replay checker must restrict}

The miniature checkers make the induction discipline a first-class, checked
datum, and this is the part a production implementation most needs to inherit.

There are separate syntactic constructors for universal pattern variables and
for \emph{rigid} free variables. In an induction step the recursion index --- a
list tail, a natural predecessor --- is rigid; only genuinely generalised
parameters remain instantiable in the induction hypothesis. Treating the tail as
an ordinary rewrite metavariable would let an induction hypothesis rewrite the
goal at an unjustified larger argument. In the base case the induction
hypothesis is not available at all.

The strongest formulation attaches to each rule an explicit \emph{flexible}
variable set --- the variables at which that rule, notably the induction
hypothesis, may be instantiated --- and requires each replayed step to supply
exactly that substitution set with correct sorts, rejecting missing or illegal
instantiations.

Every rewrite certificate contains a rule identifier and a subterm path. The
checker retrieves the rule from the defining equations, from an earlier checked
lemma, or from the current induction hypothesis; matches the left-hand side;
checks repeated-variable identity and sorts; replaces the selected subterm; and
continues. The two replayed sides must end syntactically identical. No search
and no normalisation happen during replay.

\begin{proposition}[Soundness of the list certificate format]
Assume that previously installed equations are valid for all finite lists. If
the checker accepts an induction certificate, its universally quantified
equation holds for all finite lists under the supplied definitions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each defining rewrite preserves the interpreted value. Each installed lemma
instance preserves it by assumption, and congruence permits replacement inside
a typed term context. An accepted base trace therefore proves the empty-list
case. In the step trace the only additional rule is the desired statement for
the rigid tail, universally quantified over its generalised parameters, and that
rule is valid under the structural induction hypothesis. The accepted step trace
therefore proves the constructor case, and structural induction establishes the
universal equation.
\end{proof}

This justifies a small format. It is not a machine-checked Lean proof of the
Python implementation, and the negative tests reduce rather than remove the risk
of a checker bug.

\subsubsection*{Lemma discovery and non-circularity}

A public theorem-installation operation first checks both induction cases, and
\emph{a lemma is not available while its own certificate is being checked} ---
which rejects a forged proof that cites its own conclusion. A bundle of lemmas
is accepted only in a dependency order in which no entry refers to a later one,
and shadowing a defining equation or the induction-hypothesis name is rejected
outright.

Beyond generalisation, a demand-driven lemma explorer works from the residual
of a failed step. Retain the normalised left and right frontiers and the
rewrites that produced them. Bounded equality saturation can expose alternative
forms a one-way normaliser would have discarded --- for the accumulator example,
unfolding append in reverse exposes $h::a$ as $A([h],a)$, and the remaining
frontier then suggests $A(A(u,v),w)=A(u,A(v,w))$.

\begin{lstlisting}
find_lemma(left_frontier, right_frontier):
  expand each frontier using a bounded set of proved equalities
  identify pairs separated by a small structural difference
  anti-unify shared subterms, preserving repeated-variable identity
  abstract a well-typed parameter telescope
  reject trivial, cyclic, or irrelevant candidates
  filter candidates with executable counterexamples where available
  prove surviving candidates in a separate obligation
  return only checked lemmas to the main equality state
\end{lstlisting}

Typed anti-unification starts in a restricted fragment: fully applied,
homogeneous first-order terms with compatible types. Memoise pairs of subterms
so that anti-unifying $f(a,a)$ and $f(b,b)$ gives $f(z,z)$ and not $f(z_1,z_2)$.
In a dependent fragment, an abstraction that changes a term's type needs a typed
telescope and possibly transport proofs; failing to find one is an ordinary
search failure.

Three further candidate sources are worth implementing: generalise a changing
non-structural argument; propose a distribution or homomorphism lemma such as
$R(A(xs,ys))=A(R(ys),R(xs))$ when nested calls obstruct normalisation; and
synthesise a stronger invariant containing the measure, bound, or relation the
step requires. Useful templates for the last are
\[
 f(s,a)=H(s,a),\qquad
 \mu(f(s,a))=\mu(a)+J(s),\qquad
 R(f(s,a),s,a).
\]
Each candidate becomes its own obligation, never a rewrite rule merely because
it helped simplify another candidate. Quotient syntactically different
candidates only by \emph{already proved} equalities; otherwise the quotient can
discard the correct expression or conceal an unproved assumption.

This goal-directed exploration is closely related to CCLemma's use of e-graphs
to focus inductive lemma discovery on useful proof frontiers, and to the
rippling tradition; the proposal adapts that work to a proof-producing
controller rather than originating
it~\cite{cclemma,rippling,directed-lemma-synthesis}.

Impose separate limits on term size, saturation nodes, candidate count,
induction depth, and lemma-dependency depth, and use term fingerprints and
alpha-normalised telescopes to avoid reproving the same lemma under new binder
names. Bounded enumeration is exponential in its grammar size; the production
advantage must come from recursive-call analysis, relevance filtering, typed
indexing, and frontier selection, not from assuming that unrestricted
enumeration will be cheap.

\subsection{Invariant synthesis by exact CEGIS}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python by six independent efforts, in
univariate, bivariate, multivariate, accumulator and state-machine forms.}

\subsubsection*{The additive fragment and its certificate}

Consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
T(0)=t_0,\qquad T(n+1)=T(n)+f(n),\qquad n\in\N,
\end{equation}
with $t_0\in\Q$ and $f\in\Q[X]$. Search for $q\in\Q[X]$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec-cert}
q(0)=t_0,\qquad q(X+1)-q(X)=f(X).
\end{equation}
The certificate is the exact coefficient list of $q$. Its checker computes the
shift $q(X+1)$ by polynomial arithmetic and compares exact coefficients; it does
not sample the step relation and does not trust an interpolator.

\begin{proposition}[Recurrence certificate]
\label{prop:rec}
If \eqref{eq:rec-cert} holds then $T(n)=q(n)$ for every $n\in\N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The base equality is the first condition. If $T(n)=q(n)$ then
\eqref{eq:recurrence} and the second condition give
$T(n+1)=q(n)+f(n)=q(n+1)$. Apply induction on $n$.
\end{proof}

The multivariate form carries parameters. For
$S(0,\bm a)=I(\bm a)$ and $S(n+1,\bm a)=S(n,\bm a)+P(n,\bm a)$, search for
$F(n,\bm a)=\sum_{|\alpha|\le D}c_\alpha(n,\bm a)^\alpha$, substitute into
$F(0,\bm a)=I(\bm a)$ and $F(n+1,\bm a)-F(n,\bm a)=P(n,\bm a)$, and equate
coefficients to obtain a rational linear system in the $c_\alpha$.

For the accumulator shape
$\mathrm{go}(0,a)=a$, $\mathrm{go}(n+1,a)=\mathrm{go}(n,a+r(n))$, the ansatz is
$F(n,a)=P(n)+aQ(n)$ with obligations $F(0,a)=a$ and $F(n+1,a)=F(n,a+r(n))$.
One implementation joins the two systems by introducing a formal tag variable
$t$, encoding the base equation in the coefficients of $t^0$ and the step
equation in those of $t^1$, so that a single exact row reduction solves both
simultaneously; $t$ does not appear in the final invariant.

\subsubsection*{Counterexamples direct the coefficient search}

For degree bound $d$, write $q(X)=\sum_{i=0}^d c_iX^i$, maintain exact sample
constraints $q(n)=T(n)$, and solve the rational linear system with free
coefficients set to zero. Check the candidate's base and step identities. If the
step residual
\[
r(X)=q(X+1)-q(X)-f(X)
\]
is nonzero, choose a natural $n$ with $r(n)\ne0$ and add \emph{both} $q(n)=T(n)$
and $q(n+1)=T(n+1)$ to the sample set. At least one is a new constraint: if both
were already satisfied by the candidate, their difference would force $r(n)=0$.
A nonzero degree-$k$ polynomial cannot vanish at all of $0,1,\ldots,k$, so an
exact separating counterexample is found without an SMT solver. If the
coefficient constraints become inconsistent, raise the degree bound.

\begin{proposition}[Fragment completeness]
\label{prop:rec-complete}
For a nonzero $f$ of degree $k$, the finite-difference map
$q\mapsto q(X+1)-q(X)$ sends a leading term $aX^{k+1}$ to $(k+1)aX^k$ plus lower
terms. Over $\Q$ one may therefore solve for the leading coefficient, subtract
its difference, and continue downward: a degree-$(k+1)$ solution exists and is
unique once $q(0)$ is fixed. The zero increment gives a constant solution.
Hence exact linear solving with a degree budget of at least $k+1$ terminates on
this fragment.
\end{proposition}

Equivalently, the forward-difference operator maps polynomials of degree at most
$d+1$ with zero constant term bijectively onto polynomials of degree at most
$d$, since its matrix in the monomial basis is triangular with nonzero diagonal.
In the binomial basis $\Delta\binom{n}{k+1}=\binom{n}{k}$, which a future
implementation could exploit; the prototypes use ordinary monomials and exact
row reduction because that keeps the certificate checker small.

This is a \emph{fragment-level} coverage result. It says nothing about
recurrences involving products with $S$, branching updates, nonlinear state
transformations, piecewise definitions, or integer formulas with truncated
division, and it does not make the broader induction planner complete.

\subsubsection*{The overfitting trap}

For $f(X)=X+1$ the discovered result is $q(X)=X(X+1)/2$. The interpolated
expression
\[
\widetilde q(X)=\frac{X(X+1)}2+X(X-1)(X-2)(X-3)
\]
agrees with the triangular-number formula on the first four inputs and is
rejected by the step checker. This is a small, concrete reason why conjecture
generation and proof acceptance must be separate operations, and the same trap
is built into the test suites as a hostile control: a high-degree term that
vanishes at every sampled point but violates the recurrence identity elsewhere.

For $P(n)=n^2$ with $I=0$ the synthesised result is
\[
 F(n)=\frac{n^3}{3}-\frac{n^2}{2}+\frac n6=\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}6,
\]
and one run recovered the degree-9 invariant for $r(n)=n^8$,
\[
F(n,a)=a+\frac{n^9}{9}-\frac{n^8}{2}+\frac{2n^7}{3}
 -\frac{7n^5}{15}+\frac{2n^3}{9}-\frac{n}{30}.
\]

\subsubsection*{The state-machine generalisation}

A more reusable certificate format treats the invariant as a conserved quantity
of a transition system: for initial state $s_0$ and transition $T$, require
\[
 I(s_0)=0,\qquad I(T(s))=I(s).
\]
The second identity is a convenient \emph{sufficient} condition --- proving
$I(s)=0\Rightarrow I(T(s))=0$ would also suffice but is not the implemented
certificate language. For sums of squares, clearing denominators gives
$J(n,s)=6s-n(n+1)(2n+1)$, and
\[
 J(n+1,s+(n+1)^2)-J(n,s)
 =6(n+1)^2-(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)+n(n+1)(2n+1)=0 .
\]

The next step is a genuine invariant worker for a state transition
$s'=F(n,s)$, proving the initial condition and
\[
 I(n,s)\land\mathrm{Guard}(n,s)\;\Longrightarrow\;I(n+1,F(n,s)).
\]
Coefficient comparison handles identities and exact nonlinear certificates can
handle some inequalities. A warning applies to the multi-invariant version: if
the step condition takes the form $I_i(T(\vec x))=\sum_j h_{ij}(\vec x)I_j(\vec
x)$, then jointly discovering the $I_j$ and the multipliers is a
\emph{bilinear} search, not linear algebra, and it should not be described as
the latter. Separate candidate generation from multiplier certification, or fix
one side at a time.

\begin{remark}
An invariant does not prove that a loop or a recursive definition terminates.
Termination is a separate obligation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Negative verdicts: when ``not found'' may become ``does not exist''}
\label{sec:negative}

Everywhere else in this document a bounded search that finds nothing returns
\unknown{}, and that rule is not negotiable. But \unknown{} is not the only
possible negative answer, and treating it as such gives up a real capability. A
tactic that can sometimes report \emph{provably no such term exists} is doing
something \code{exact?}, \code{tauto}, Aesop, \code{decide} and \code{omega} do
not (\S\ref{sec:tactic-gap}) --- and something a user acts on very differently
from a timeout.

Exactly one worker in this collection can already do it: integer-lattice
elimination emits a divisibility obstruction that \emph{proves} no integer
solution exists (Proposition~\ref{prop:lattice}). The question is what makes
that legitimate, and what else could qualify.

\subsubsection*{Exhausted is not truncated}

The first requirement is a distinction the worker contract of
\S\ref{sec:architecture} does not draw. \code{Unknown(reason)} conflates two
outcomes that are logically different:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{exhausted} --- the search space was covered and contained no
  solution;
\item \textbf{truncated} --- a budget, depth cap, queue bound, candidate
  cutoff, or omitted plan stopped the search early.
\end{itemize}

Only the first can support a negative conclusion, and a worker must therefore
report which occurred rather than leaving the caller to guess. A useful shape
for a resumable search is a small-step machine whose event type makes the
distinction unforgeable:

\begin{lstlisting}
inductive SearchEvent where
  | found     (evidence : Certificate) (next : Machine)
  | stepped   (next : Machine)             -- administrative progress
  | exhausted (summary : ExhaustionSummary)
  | truncated (reason  : TruncationReason)
\end{lstlisting}

with the invariants that every transition consumes its documented choice count;
that administrative steps cannot be mistaken for exhaustion; that a truncated
branch contributes nothing to global negative evidence; and that a plan
scheduler shares a global budget rather than resetting a local one per plan.
That last invariant is the same one \S\ref{sec:architecture} states for nested
actions, and it is load-bearing here: a search whose sub-searches each reset
their own budget can report \code{exhausted} having covered very little.

\subsubsection*{The completeness ledger}

Exhaustion of the search is necessary but not sufficient. The \emph{problem}
handed to the search must also have been a faithful rendering of the goal, and
that is a separate obligation with its own failure modes: an unsupported
subterm silently abstracted to an atom, a family description truncated at a
recursion bound, an instance binder dropped, a plan omitted from the
enumeration.

The mechanism that makes this checkable is a \emph{completeness ledger}: a data
structure carried alongside the reified problem whose constructors name exactly
which obligation is missing. It is not a Boolean. Any entry in it weakens a
no-solution result to \unknown{}, and because its constructors are named, the
diagnostic can say \emph{why} the negative verdict was withheld.

Two implementation properties make this work, and both are cheap.

First, the ledger is computed by the \emph{encoder}, at translation time,
before any search runs. It is a property of the rendering, not of the outcome,
so it cannot be influenced by how the search went. A reference implementation
carries it as one bit per translated goal, set at each site that loses
information: a quantifier in negative position sealed as an opaque atom, a
recursive datatype unfolded only to a bounded depth, a class context accepted
without its dictionary premises.

Second, the bit is carried \emph{per node}, not only per problem. Every atom in
the reified goal records whether it is safe for refutation. An atom built
purely from universally quantified variables keeps a negative verdict sound; an
atom mentioning a concrete constant, whose structure the engine cannot see,
poisons it. So does an erased typeclass dictionary --- because the dictionary
can carry data, and a term inhabiting the goal may exist by way of a method the
search never modelled.

The payoff is a diagnostic that names the obstruction. Rather than ``no term
found within bounds'', the user can be told \emph{which symbols} blocked the
stronger claim --- which is the difference between an unhelpful timeout and an
actionable one.

The combined predicate is then a conjunction, checked in a fixed order:
\[
\text{search completed} \;\wedge\;
\text{translation lossless} \;\wedge\;
\text{no self-reference available} .
\]
The third conjunct is worth keeping. If the goal's own name was excluded from
the premises during search --- as it must be, to prevent a circular proof ---
then a negative result can be sharpened by one extra bounded search that
reintroduces it, paid for out of the first search's unused budget. Succeeding
there yields the genuinely useful message \emph{``no non-recursive inhabitant
exists; one exists if the definition may call itself''}. The discipline that
makes this safe is monotonicity: the sharper claim may degrade to the weaker
claim, but exhausting the budget during the diagnostic pass must never degrade
an already-decided result to \unknown{}.

\begin{invariant}[Negative evidence requires an intact chain]
\label{inv:negative}
A ``provably no solution'' verdict requires \emph{both} that the search
reported \code{exhausted}, \emph{and} that the completeness ledger for the
reified problem is empty. Failure of either yields \unknown{}. The display layer
must never upgrade \unknown{} to a refutation.
\end{invariant}

For a term-synthesis worker over a dependently typed goal this specialises to a
two-axis rule: a negative verdict requires that no quantified occurrence was
left opaque or bounded-instantiated, \emph{and} that no atom is hiding
dependent structure. Otherwise the answer is ``no term found within bounds''.

\subsubsection*{Portfolio asymmetry}

A portfolio that mixes a complete worker with incomplete ones needs an explicit
rule, or the incomplete ones will corrupt the complete one's verdicts. The rule
is asymmetric:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item An incomplete worker's \emph{candidates} improve coverage and ranking.
\item An incomplete worker's \emph{failure} can neither create nor cancel a
  refutation.
\item A complete worker's negative evidence survives only if its own
  fragment, translation and search completeness chain is intact
  (Invariant~\ref{inv:negative}).
\item A wrapper that transforms the problem --- a classical translation, a
  strengthened goal --- produces a \emph{new} problem, and must drop any
  evidence that no longer denotes it.
\end{itemize}

Concretely: a Bernstein worker timing out says nothing about a goal that the
Sturm worker of \S\ref{sec:sturm} decides, and the scheduler must not let the
first result suppress the second.

\subsubsection*{What could qualify}

Three further sources of genuine negative evidence are available to this
design, and only the first is implemented.

\emph{Lattice obstructions} (implemented). $g \nmid r$ after unimodular
elimination proves that $Ax=b$ has no integer solution. The certificate is
replayable and the checker verifies $\det U=\pm1$ independently.

\emph{Checked countermodels} (partially implemented). An exact rational point
at which the target is negative, \emph{validated independently} for membership
in the domain and for sign, refutes a nonnegativity goal. One Bernstein
implementation already returns such a point as a three-valued
\textsc{refuted} outcome with a separate \code{check\_witness}. The discipline
that makes this sound is worth stating as a formula: a solver's output is not
semantic evidence, and only
\[
\text{sealed problem} \;+\; \text{canonical query} \;+\;
\text{bounded observation} \;+\; \text{independent replay}
\]
produces a countermodel receipt. A satisfying assignment to an abstraction
remains a \code{Candidate}.

\emph{Intuitionistic propositional inhabitation} (not implemented; the largest
available addition). A terminating decision procedure for the implicational
propositional fragment --- Dyckhoff's contraction-free \textsf{LJT} being the
standard choice --- decides inhabitation rather than merely searching for it.
Within its fragment, exhaustion \emph{is} non-inhabitation.

It is worth being exact about the \emph{strength} of that, because it is not
the same as the lattice worker's. A reference implementation of this design
exists and produces, for a negative answer, a single enumeration tag and no
artefact: there is no closed-branch tree, no refutation object, nothing a
second program can re-check. Its soundness rests on the decision procedure
being correctly implemented, plus the translation gate below. So it belongs in
a \emph{weaker} slot than lattice elimination, which hands back a certificate
whose checker verifies $\det U=\pm1$ without calling the elimination that
produced $U$.

\emph{Finite Kripke countermodels} (implemented). The gap just described is
real but it is not the only route, and the alternative turns out to be both
smaller and easier to check. Rather than reifying a failed search tree, exhibit
a \emph{finite model} in which the hypotheses hold and the goal does not.

A model is a finite set of worlds with a least root $w_0$, a partial order, and
one Boolean valuation per atom, subject to \emph{persistence}: if $w\le v$ and
an atom holds at $w$, it holds at $v$. Forcing is the usual Kripke semantics,
\begin{align*}
 w\Vdash P &\iff P\text{ holds at }w, &
 w&\nVdash\bot, \\
 w\Vdash A\land B &\iff w\Vdash A\text{ and }w\Vdash B, &
 w\Vdash A\lor B &\iff w\Vdash A\text{ or }w\Vdash B, \\
 w\Vdash A\to B &\iff \forall v\ge w,\ v\Vdash A\Rightarrow v\Vdash B.
\end{align*}
The implication clause must quantify over \emph{successors}; evaluating it at
the current world alone collapses the semantics to something essentially
classical and is simply the wrong check.

The certificate is accepted exactly when the order and persistence conditions
hold, every assumption in $\Gamma$ is forced at the root, and the goal is not.
The checker \emph{recomputes forcing}; a truth table supplied alongside the
model is unnecessary and untrusted.

\begin{lemma}[Persistence]
If $w\le v$ and $w\Vdash A$ then $v\Vdash A$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Induct on $A$. Atoms use valuation persistence and $\bot$ is never forced;
conjunction and disjunction use the induction hypotheses. For implication, let
$t\ge v$; transitivity gives $t\ge w$, so the implication forced at $w$ applies
at $t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Countermodel soundness]
\label{thm:kripke}
An accepted model for $\Gamma$ and $G$ rules out any derivation of $G$ from
$\Gamma$ in intuitionistic natural deduction.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Show by induction on a derivation that every world forcing its assumptions
forces its conclusion. Assumptions, the conjunction rules and the disjunction
introductions are immediate. Disjunction elimination uses the forced disjunct
at the current world and the induction hypothesis for the corresponding branch.
Implication elimination applies the forcing clause with $v=w$ by reflexivity.
For implication introduction, move to an arbitrary successor forcing the
antecedent; persistence carries the undischarged assumptions there and the
induction hypothesis gives the consequent. Falsity elimination is vacuous, as
no world forces $\bot$. A derivation of $G$ would therefore force $G$ at the
root, contradicting the checked model.
\end{proof}

This \emph{is} the checkable refutation object, and it is small: a relation
matrix and a valuation, with a checker that re-evaluates forcing bottom-up.

\begin{remark}[The semantic ceiling, and why it is load-bearing]
\label{rem:kripke-ceiling}
What an accepted model establishes is precisely \emph{no derivation of $G$ from
$\Gamma$ in the named object calculus}. It does \emph{not} mean that Lean can
prove $\neg G$, that no Lean term of $G$ exists, or that classical reasoning
cannot prove $G$. It says nothing about domain hypotheses omitted from the
object sequent.

The classical tautologies are the enforcement tests, not decoration. Excluded
middle $P\lor\neg P$ falls to two worlds $0<1$ with $P$ false at the root and
true above: the root forces neither disjunct. The same chain refutes
$\neg\neg P\to P$, and with $Q$ false everywhere it refutes Peirce's formula
$((P\to Q)\to P)\to P$. All three are classically valid. An interface that
turned these models into proofs of the \emph{negated Lean formulas} would be
visibly unsound, and keeping them in the corpus is what stops that interface
from being built by accident.
\end{remark}

Two further points of design. Chains are not enough: with a root and two
incomparable successors making $P$ and $Q$ true respectively, neither $P\to Q$
nor $Q\to P$ holds at the root, so $(P\to Q)\lor(Q\to P)$ is refuted --- and no
chain of any length refutes it. A proposer should enumerate rooted trees and a
checker should accept arbitrary rooted finite partial orders.

And the bound is a bound. When the world budget is exhausted the result is
\unknown{}. Valid schemas in a corpus are \emph{positive controls against an
invalid refuter}, not theorems proved by the absence of a small countermodel:
that intuitionistic propositional logic has the finite model property does not
turn a three-world cap into a decision procedure. The same applies to a SAT
backend for the model search~\cite{pysat}: such a solver is a replaceable
\emph{proposer}, its satisfying assignment is decoded into the ordinary
certificate and independently checked, and its \code{unsat} at fixed $n$
carries no unbounded authority --- which remains true even if the solver is
correct.

\begin{remark}[Composition with the classical fallback]
Theorem~\ref{thm:kripke} and Glivenko's theorem answer complementary halves of
the same question, and together they give a complete propositional verdict. A
countermodel says $G$ is not constructively derivable; the double-negation
search then says whether it is classically provable, and returns the witness if
so. Peirce's formula is exactly the case where the first says \emph{no} and the
second says \emph{yes, here it is} --- which is far more useful to a user than
either answer alone.
\end{remark}

Two properties make this unusually good value here. First, it operates on the
\emph{propositional skeleton} of a goal, so dependent subformulas that only
need to be transported rather than analysed can be alpha-normalised to opaque
atoms and the goal still decided: $(\forall n:\N,\,P\,n)\to Q\to(\forall
n:\N,\,P\,n)$ is closed by $\lambda h\,\_\Rightarrow h$ without the worker ever
looking inside $P$. Second, it composes with a classical fallback that is
complete for the same fragment. By Glivenko's theorem, a propositional formula
is classically provable exactly when its double negation is intuitionistically
provable, so re-running the same decision procedure on $\neg\neg G$ decides
classical propositional provability, and a resulting term
$t:\neg G\to\bot$ reconstructs as \code{Classical.byContradiction t}.
Peirce's law is the standard illustration: constructively uninhabited, and the
fallback supplies the classical witness. The honesty condition is that
\emph{quantified goals skip the fallback}, since Glivenko does not extend past
the propositional fragment without double-negation shifts.

\subsection{Witness synthesis: construct terms, not satisfying assignments}

\subsubsection*{The logical distinction}

To prove $\forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y)$, a satisfying assignment for one value
of $x$ is not enough. A useful synthesiser produces a term $y=w(x)$ and proves
$\forall x\,P(x,w(x))$. For
\[
\forall x\in\R,\quad \exists y\in\R,\quad x+1\le y\le x+2,
\]
the witness is $w(x)=x+1$; no constant works for every real $x$, and a
constant-only template returns no certificate where the affine template
succeeds.

Sampling individual values of $x$ and finding unrelated values of $y$
constructs no function and proves nothing. The grammar must represent a
well-typed expression $w(x)$. A synthesis oracle may help choose $w$; it cannot
justify $P(x,w(x))$ by testing.

Witness binders retain their dependencies. A witness for the second existential
may refer to an earlier witness but not to a future binder or to a branch-local
variable outside its scope. The instructive negative example is
$\exists x:\alpha,\forall y:\alpha,\;x=y$: the witness for $x$ cannot be the
later-bound $y$, and a naive synthesiser that sees all displayed variables will
propose exactly that. The existential task's context must exclude $y$, and
replay must reject any escaped local variable.

Constructing a value of \code{Fin n} requires a value \emph{and} a bound proof;
over $\N$, a construction using a signed intermediate representation carries a
nonnegativity obligation. A witness is a program with a proof obligation.

The search grammar should be type-directed and size-bounded: local variables,
constructors, projections, selected recursive calls, affine combinations, and
guarded division or remainder operations. Piecewise terms are permitted only
with exhaustive, proved branch conditions --- never on the assumption that
finitely many sampled regions cover the domain.

\subsubsection*{Affine witnesses with Farkas multipliers}
\label{sec:affine-witness}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python.}

Let the input domain be the rational polyhedron $g(x)=Ax+b\ge0$ and the target
constraints $h(x,y)=Cx+Dy+e\ge0$, all componentwise and non-strict, and seek
$y=Mx+c$. A sufficient certificate is a nonnegative matrix $\Lambda$ and a
nonnegative vector $s$ with
\begin{align}
C+DM&=\Lambda A,\label{eq:farkas-linear}\\
e+Dc&=\Lambda b+s.\label{eq:farkas-constant}
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Affine witness certificate]
If \eqref{eq:farkas-linear}--\eqref{eq:farkas-constant} hold with
$\Lambda,s\ge0$, then for every $x$ with $Ax+b\ge0$ the term $y=Mx+c$ satisfies
$Cx+Dy+e\ge0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substitution gives $Cx+D(Mx+c)+e=\Lambda(Ax+b)+s$. Every entry on the right is a
sum of nonnegative multiples of nonnegative quantities plus a nonnegative slack.
\end{proof}

The decisive observation is that \eqref{eq:farkas-linear} and
\eqref{eq:farkas-constant} are \emph{linear in the unknowns} $M,c,\Lambda,s$,
because $A,b,C,D,e$ are fixed problem data. Witness synthesis and proof search
are therefore \emph{one} linear feasibility problem rather than an enumeration
of candidate affine functions followed by a separate verification pass. With $n$
inputs, $m$ outputs, $r$ domain constraints and $q$ target constraints the
encoding has $m(n+1)+q(r+1)$ unknowns and $q(n+1)$ coefficient equations before
template restrictions; entries of $M,c$ are unrestricted and entries of
$\Lambda,s$ are constrained nonnegative. A constant-only grammar adds $M=0$.

For a nonempty rational polyhedron, the affine implication form of Farkas'
lemma gives a completeness route for a \emph{fixed} affine witness: validity of
each non-strict affine target inequality has such
multipliers~\cite{mosek-cookbook}. The floating-point proposer is not a complete
exact LP algorithm and an empty domain needs separate handling, but neither
qualification affects certificate soundness.

A variant fragment solves $A w=Bx+c$ for $w=Wx+d$ by coefficient matching,
giving the exact systems $AW=B$ and $Ad=c$, each solved by rational reduced
row-echelon elimination with free variables set to zero, and re-multiplied
exactly by the checker with no sampled values of $x$ involved.

\subsubsection*{Integer witnesses are a different problem}

A rational affine solution is not an integer proof. The implemented integer mode
merely requires every coefficient of $W$ and $d$ to be integral, and is
deliberately incomplete: a different choice of free variables might yield an
integral solution when the default rational one does not. The instructive case
is $2w=x$, which has the rational witness $x/2$ and no integer witness valid for
every integer $x$; an integer solver must obtain a parity assumption or fail,
and must never silently reinterpret rational division as integer arithmetic.

A practical first integer grammar is
\[
 w(x) ::= c+\sum_i a_ix_i\;\Big|\;
 \left\lfloor\frac{c+\sum_i a_ix_i}{d}\right\rfloor\;\Big|\;
 \operatorname{ite}(g,w_1,w_2),\qquad d>0,
\]
with every candidate checked in the source integer semantics.

\subsubsection*{Integer lattices and certificates of impossibility}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python. The only worker here that emits a
certificate of infeasibility.}

A stronger integer fragment solves $Ax=b$ over $\Z$ by row-by-row unimodular
elimination, returning either a particular solution with a basis for all
homogeneous freedom, or a replayable divisibility obstruction. It does not
search a bounded numerical box. For $6x+10y=2$ it returns
\[
(x,y)=(2,-1)+t(-5,3),\qquad t\in\Z,
\]
and the trace explains why this family contains \emph{all} solutions --- which
is what a later impossibility argument needs.

After processing rows $0,\ldots,i-1$, maintain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lattice}
\{x\in\Z^n:A_{<i}x=b_{<i}\}=\{x_0+Bz:z\in\Z^d\},
\end{equation}
initially $x_0=0$, $B=I_n$, $d=n$. For the next row $a$ compute $v=aB$ and
$r=b_i-ax_0$, and find an integer unimodular $U$ with
$vU=(g,0,\ldots,0)$, $g\ge0$. If $g=0$, consistency requires $r=0$ and the
family is unchanged. If $g>0$, consistency requires $g\mid r$; with $q=r/g$,
update
\[
x_0\leftarrow x_0+(BU)_{:,0}\,q,\qquad
B\leftarrow(BU)_{:,1:},\qquad d\leftarrow d-1 .
\]
The elementary block, for a current pair $(a,b)$ with $sa+tb=g$, is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}s&-b/g\\t&a/g\end{pmatrix},
\]
whose determinant is $(sa+tb)/g=1$ and which sends the pair to $(g,0)$; a sign
flip makes the final first entry nonnegative.

\begin{proposition}[Lattice elimination]
\label{prop:lattice}
Each successful row update preserves \eqref{eq:lattice}, and a reported
divisibility obstruction proves that no integer solution exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Because $U$ is unimodular, $z=Uw$ is a bijection of $\Z^d$. The new row equation
becomes $gw_0=r$. For $g>0$ this has an integer solution precisely when
$g\mid r$, in which case $w_0=r/g$ and all other coordinates remain free;
substitution gives the stated update. For $g=0$ the row is either redundant or
contradictory. Induction over processed rows proves both claims.
\end{proof}

The replay format stores, per row, the row number, $U$, $g$, $r$, and either the
quotient or an obstruction marker. The independent checker recomputes the
current row image and residual, checks that all entries are integers, verifies
$\det U=\pm1$, and performs the exact state update --- without calling the
extended-gcd construction or the solver. For $[6\ 10]$ one trace step is
\[
U=\begin{pmatrix}2&-5\\-1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
[6\ 10]\,U=[2\ 0],\qquad \det U=1,
\]
and with right-hand side $1$ the same reduction establishes the obstruction
$2\nmid1$.

A production Lean checker might instead accept elementary column operations or
an explicitly checked integer inverse; the elementary-operation format is
attractive because it has short local correctness lemmas and avoids determinant
expansion. For a positive existential goal, checking a returned witness directly
is cheaper than verifying completeness of the whole lattice --- the latter is
needed only when the family or a negative result is used.

Parameterised right-hand sides extend the same machinery: if $Au_0=b_0$ and
$AU=C$, then $x=u_0+Up$ is a witness family for $Ax=b_0+Cp$. This sufficient
method misses witnesses that need piecewise divisibility conditions or integer
division, so it must not report impossibility when the coefficientwise
construction fails. Carrying affine residuals through the same row algorithm
produces obligations $g\mid r(p)$ and a checked integer quotient once they are
proved --- for example, $2x=p$ requires evenness of $p$. If the goal also
includes $Cx\le d$, substitute the lattice parameterisation to obtain
inequalities in the remaining integer parameters; \emph{dropping the
inequalities after solving the equalities would be unsound}, and
natural-number witnesses additionally require nonnegativity, since solutions
over $\Z$ do not automatically inhabit $\N$.

There are at most $n$ dimension-reducing steps, but intermediate coefficient
bit lengths rather than matrix dimensions can dominate cost. The prototype is a
straightforward exact implementation, not an optimised Smith or Hermite
normal-form library.

\subsubsection*{Finite-residue witnesses}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python.}

A different existential shape is discharged by finite case analysis on a residue
class. Given a modulus $m$, synthesise offsets $d_r$, one per residue class
$r$ modulo $m$, such that for every integer $n$,
\[
 n\le n+d_{n\bmod m}<n+m
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 a\,(r+d_r)\equiv b \pmod m .
\]
Verification is a finite check over the $m$ residue classes; the universal
interpretation is justified by the quotient/remainder theorem, not by the
sampled integers. When the congruence $a\,z\equiv b\pmod m$ has no solution ---
detected by a gcd divisibility condition --- the worker reports impossibility for
that parameter tuple rather than failing silently. The implementation is
exercised at inputs such as $10^{20}+7$ to confirm that the finite table really
does instantiate at arbitrary integers.

\subsubsection*{A straight-line witness program instead of a residue table}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python \src{e8}; no Lean discharge.}

The finite-residue worker above pays a cost proportional to the modulus. A
modulus with a short binary representation can therefore be unaffordable: a
period of $10^{18}$ is eighteen digits to write down and $10^{18}$ table rows to
enumerate. The extension round replaces the table with an exact
\emph{projection}. For the one-output conjunctive fragment
\[
 \bigwedge_p a_p\,y\le\beta_p(x),
 \qquad
 \bigwedge_c c_c\,y\equiv\delta_c(x)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m_c),
\]
with integer coefficients and affine parameter right sides $\beta,\delta$, the
worker returns a pair: a quantifier-free \emph{guard} $C(x)$ and a
straight-line \emph{witness program} $w(x)$, such that
\[
 \exists y\in\Z,\ \Phi(x,y)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 C(x),
 \qquad\text{and } C(x)\Rightarrow\Phi(x,w(x)).
\]

Three steps produce it. First, each congruence coefficient is normalised: with
$g=\gcd(c,m)>0$ and $uc+vm=g$, a solution exists exactly when $g\mid\delta$, and
then $y\equiv u(\delta/g)\pmod{m/g}$. The quotient is an exact integer quotient
\emph{under the guard}; the program computes it by floor division but never
discards the guard, so the residue expression stays defined even when the guard
is false. Second, the normalised congruences are merged pairwise. This is where
a textbook Chinese remainder step is not enough, because the moduli are neither
prime nor pairwise coprime.

\begin{lemma}[Guarded merge]\label{lem:crt}
Let $h=\gcd(M,N)$ with $pM+qN=h$, let
$k=\bigl(p\,(s-r)/h\bigr)\bmod (N/h)$, let $M'=\operatorname{lcm}(M,N)$ and
$r'=(r+Mk)\bmod M'$. Then
\[
 \bigl(y\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ M)\ \wedge\ y\equiv s\ (\mathrm{mod}\ N)\bigr)
 \iff
 \bigl(h\mid (s-r)\ \wedge\ y\equiv r'\ (\mathrm{mod}\ M')\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If both congruences hold, write $y-r=M\ell$ and $y-s=Nj$; then
$s-r=M\ell-Nj$, so $h\mid(s-r)$. Divide by $h$: the equation for $\ell$ modulo
$N/h$ has inverse $p$ for $M/h$, because $p(M/h)+q(N/h)=1$. Hence
$\ell\equiv k\pmod{N/h}$, which is exactly $y\equiv r+Mk\pmod{M'}$. Conversely
compatibility with that congruence gives the first congruence directly and the
second by substituting the definition of $k$. Reducing $r+Mk$ modulo $M'$ does
not change its class.
\end{proof}

Third, the inequalities become an interval. With $a>0$ the bound is
$y\le\lfloor\beta/a\rfloor$; with $a=-c<0$ it is $y\ge-\lfloor\beta/c\rfloor$,
using $\lceil-\beta/c\rceil=-\lfloor\beta/c\rfloor$ for $c>0$; with $a=0$ the
row degenerates to a parameter guard $0\le\beta$. The negative case is the one
that is easy to get wrong, and truncation toward zero gives wrong answers at
negative parameters. There are no sentinel values for a missing endpoint: the
problem is over unbounded integers, not a machine range. Finally, intersect the
arithmetic progression with the interval. With a lower endpoint,
\[
 w=L+\bigl((r-L)\bmod M\bigr)
\]
is the least congruent integer at least $L$, so an upper endpoint is satisfiable
exactly when $w\le U$; with only an upper endpoint,
$w=U-((U-r)\bmod M)$ always works and needs no extra guard; with neither, $r$
itself suffices.

\begin{theorem}[Constructive projection]\label{thm:projection}
The conjunction of the normalisation guards, the merge guards of
Lemma~\ref{lem:crt}, the zero-coefficient inequality guards, and the optional
$w\le U$ guard is equivalent to $\exists y\,\Phi(x,y)$, and $w$ is a witness
whenever it holds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Normalisation and Lemma~\ref{lem:crt} give an equivalence between the original
congruences and their guards together with a single $y\equiv r\pmod M$. The
inequality transformations are equivalences over $\Z$, including the
zero-coefficient rows. What remains is to intersect one arithmetic progression
with one possibly unbounded interval. With a lower endpoint, $w$ is congruent to
$r$ and lies in $[L,L+M)$, and every other congruent integer at least $L$
differs from it by a nonnegative multiple of $M$; so an upper endpoint is
satisfiable precisely when $w\le U$. The other two endpoint cases are immediate.
These arguments establish both the feasibility equivalence and the witness, for
every parameter valuation.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The checker runs the opposite algorithm --- except where it does not.}
Each $\gcd$ arrives as a \emph{B\'ezout receipt} $(g,u,v)$, and the checker does
not run the extended Euclidean algorithm. It verifies $g>0$, $g\mid c$,
$g\mid m$, and $uc+vm=g$; those four conditions already force $g$ to be the
positive gcd, since every common divisor divides the displayed combination while
$g$ is itself a common divisor. That much is a genuine asymmetry. The rest is
not, and the source proposal says so rather than claiming otherwise: the
replayer reconstructs the witness program deterministically from the input with
\emph{the same assembler the search used} and compares. Replay therefore detects
a modified receipt or a modified program, but cannot by itself detect an
arithmetic mistake common to both uses of the assembler. The risk is addressed
by a different route --- a direct constraint evaluator and an exhaustive
concrete oracle --- not by pretending the checker is independent. This is the
weakest producer/checker separation anywhere in the thirty-six proposals. It is
disclosed as such by the proposal that built it, which is the behaviour
Section~\ref{sec:trust} asks for.

\paragraph{The program is a DAG, not a term.} Nodes are
\code{const}, \code{var}, \code{scale}, \code{add}, \code{div}, \code{mod}
(positive divisor only), \code{max} and \code{min}; guards are divisibility
tests or comparisons of node values. There is no floating-point operation, no
variable denominator, and no implicit conversion to machine integers. Shared
affine expressions and intermediate residues are hash-consed rather than copied,
so for fixed parameter arity the node count grows linearly in the number of
constraints: assembling affine right sides costs $O((p+q)(n+1))$ nodes for $p$
inequalities, $q$ congruences and $n$ parameters. The final period may be a
product of many moduli, but its bit length is at most the sum of theirs; no loop
runs once per residue class.

The recorded instance makes the contrast concrete. With
$m_1=1\,000\,000\,007$, $m_2=1\,000\,000\,009$, and constraints
$x\le y\le x+M-1$, $y\equiv 2x+1\pmod{m_1}$, $y\equiv 5x-3\pmod{m_2}$, the
period is $M=m_1m_2=1\,000\,000\,016\,000\,000\,063$ and the generated program
has \emph{42 nodes}. The interval contains exactly one representative of every
residue class, so the theorem gives feasibility for all $x$; at $x=0$ the
witness is $2\,000\,000\,015$. The program was also evaluated at
$x=\pm10^{100}$. Those large evaluations are implementation tests: the universal
claim comes from Theorem~\ref{thm:projection}, not from their size or number.
And a residue table for this period is not a sensible representation --- which
is a statement about representation size, not a measured speed comparison
against the residue worker above.

\paragraph{What it does not cover.} Variable coefficients such as $xy\le z$,
variable moduli, nonlinear parameter right sides, general Boolean combinations,
and several mutually constrained existential outputs are all outside the
implemented fragment. Disequalities would need explicit branching in a later
layer; they are not silently dropped. A one-output completeness theorem must not
be advertised as complete Presburger synthesis. For a natural-number output,
add $0\le y$ and prove that the selected integer transports to \code{Nat}; for
rational or real parameters this integer theorem is simply not the right
semantics. Quantifier order matters too: a witness may depend only on the
parameters bound before it. In the negative direction, a parameter valuation
violating $C$ is evidence only after the domain hypothesis has been checked and
the projection equivalence replayed --- a raw solver model for an unrelated
abstraction refutes nothing about the original goal.

\subsubsection*{The same construction over an ordered field}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python \src{r3}; no Lean discharge.}

The projection above is over $\Z$, and its difficulty is arithmetic:
divisibility, B\'ezout receipts, a period. Over an ordered field the arithmetic
difficulty vanishes and a different one takes its place --- \emph{strictness}.

The fragment is a conjunction of affine constraints, mixing $\le$ and $<$, with
one or more real outputs. Eliminating an output is Fourier--Motzkin: pair every
lower bound with every upper bound. The witness is then a midpoint, and the two
facts that make this work are that a midpoint exists between any two distinct
rationals --- so no analogue of the integer period is needed --- and that
mixing a strict with a non-strict bound yields a strict pair, which the
bookkeeping must track rather than round away.

Three things distinguish this from the affine-template witnesses of
\S\ref{sec:affine-witness}, and the first is the reason the worker exists at
all. Some valid linear specifications have \emph{no affine witness}. Spending
more search time on a coefficient template cannot repair that; the specification
is satisfiable at every parameter value, but no single affine function of the
parameters works. The recorded impossibility example is
$\max(0,x+y-1)$ --- a four-corner obstruction, where the four vertices force
four inconsistent affine constraints on any candidate.

The witness is therefore not an affine expression but a \emph{$\min/\max$ DAG}
over affine leaves, shared across outputs. Second, elimination is run in both
directions and the receipt records \emph{complete pair coverage}: a checker
that confirms only the pairs the search happened to use would accept a witness
valid on part of the region. Third, the negative result is a genuine semantic
object --- a rational parameter value satisfying the domain under which no
output exists at all --- rather than an exhausted search.

\begin{remark}[Midpoint reconstruction is not an integer operation]
This worker does not subsume the integer one, and the effort says so plainly.
The midpoint of two integers need not be an integer, and the whole
B\'ezout/CRT apparatus of the previous subsection exists because over $\Z$
there is no such move. The two workers cover ordered fields and the integers
respectively; neither is the other's special case.
\end{remark}
\subsubsection*{Polynomial witnesses certified by nonnegativity}
\status{Implemented and tested in Python.}

The composition pattern the whole architecture argues for appears in miniature
here. For
\[
 \forall x\in\R,\quad \exists y\in\R,\quad x\le y\ \wedge\ -x\le y,
\]
one prototype enumerates $w(x)=a+bx+cx^2$ with $a,b,c\in\{-2,\ldots,2\}$,
ordered by coefficient $\ell_1$ size and then lexicographically, using five
sample values only as a rejection filter. The 23rd candidate is $w(x)=x^2+1$,
and acceptance requires \emph{two} nonnegativity certificates, for $w-x\ge0$ and
$w+x\ge0$. Convenient explanatory decompositions are
\[
 x^2+1-x=\left(x-\tfrac12\right)^2+\tfrac34,\qquad
 x^2+1+x=\left(x+\tfrac12\right)^2+\tfrac34,
\]
but the recorded certificates contain the different rational weighted-square
decompositions the LP actually returned; both replay exactly. The artefact does
not pretend that a hand-simplified certificate was the optimiser's output.

A production implementation should also try simpler witnesses --- an available
absolute-value or maximum expression --- when the local library makes those
cheap. Restricting a prototype to polynomials is an experiment design, not a
recommended restriction on all \forge{} witnesses.

\subsection{Synthesising into a hole, not into a whole term}
\label{sec:holes}

The witness synthesis above builds first-order objects. The harder case is a
\emph{function}, and the extension round arrives at it from a specific
diagnostic rather than from a wish list. In the neighbouring Leant project, a
bounded type-directed query for list indexing produced 256 checked candidates
and falsified all of them. Separate probes then found, easily, both the
function-valued fold the answer needs and the integer case analysis the answer
needs: the step appeared at candidate 5 in an isolated context and at candidate
219 in a larger one. Neither number is that step's ordinal inside the original
whole-term search, and the reports say so.

The conclusion is not ``add function types to the carrier grammar''. They were
already there. The conclusion is that the pieces are cheap and the
\emph{composition} is not, because a whole-term enumerator pays the product of
the hole grammars while a contract-directed one pays their sum.

\subsubsection*{The proof rule chooses the holes}

Fix a carrier $B$ and a relation
$\mathcal R:\operatorname{List}(A)\to B\to\mathrm{Prop}$. The two obligations
\begin{align}
 &\mathcal R([],b_0),\label{eq:fold-base}\\
 &\forall a,\ell,b,\quad \mathcal R(\ell,b)\Longrightarrow
   \mathcal R(a::\ell,\operatorname{step}(a,b))\label{eq:fold-step}
\end{align}
imply, by structural induction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fold-rule}
 \forall\ell,\quad
 \mathcal R\bigl(\ell,\operatorname{foldr}(\operatorname{step},b_0,\ell)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Register \eqref{eq:fold-rule} once as an ordinary theorem. The synthesis task is
then to build $b_0$ and $\operatorname{step}$ meeting
\eqref{eq:fold-base}--\eqref{eq:fold-step} --- not to build a recursive program
from a bare outer type.

The relation is the load-bearing part, and it is what distinguishes this from
type-directed synthesis with a richer type language~\cite{synquid,tygar}.
Choosing the combinator and leaving its holes constrained only by \emph{types}
reproduces the original ambiguity exactly; asking a solver to discover an arbitrary expressive relation is not yet
an algorithm. A first implementation should carry an explicit inventory of
specification templates: equality against a reference fold, a size equation, a
pointwise relation for function carriers, a predicate preserved by the
constructors, or a packet of arithmetic invariants handed over from
Section~\ref{sec:closure}.

\subsubsection*{Carrier recovery, and the contracts it forces}

Indexing is the worked case. The reference operation is
\[
 \operatorname{at}(d,[],i)=d,\qquad
 \operatorname{at}(d,a::\ell,i)=
 \begin{cases}
  d,&i<0,\\
  a,&i=0,\\
  \operatorname{at}(d,\ell,i-1),&i>0.
 \end{cases}
\]
The non-structural argument $i$ changes along the recursion, so it moves
\emph{inside} the carrier: with default $d:A$, last application to $i:\Z$ and
result $A$, the carrier is $B=\Z\to A$, and a right fold needs
$b_0:\Z\to A$ and $\operatorname{step}:A\to(\Z\to A)\to(\Z\to A)$. This is the
functional counterpart of generalising an accumulator before induction, except
that the search object is a local algebra rather than a strengthened theorem.

With $\mathcal R(\ell,k)\equiv\forall i,\ k(i)=\operatorname{at}(d,\ell,i)$, the
two obligations become exact local contracts:
\begin{align}
 b_0(i)&=d,\label{eq:base-contract}\\
 \operatorname{step}(h,k)(i)&=
 \begin{cases}
  d,&i<0,\\
  h,&i=0,\\
  k(i-1),&i>0,
 \end{cases}\label{eq:step-contract}
\end{align}
where $k$ is \emph{arbitrary}. Treating $k$ as arbitrary is the decisive
choice. A candidate step is not permitted to exploit the behaviour of one
guessed tail function that happens to fit a handful of examples.

The carrier heuristic generalises but does not become a theorem: when a
non-structural argument changes in a recursive specification and the result type
is $R$, try $I\to R$; for several changing arguments, try a curried or product
carrier in the original dependency order. That is an alternative to enqueue, not
a claim that every such function is a fold.

\subsubsection*{Ghost variables: the proof context is larger than the code context}

Write $\Gamma_{\mathrm{code}}=(d,a,k,i)$ and
$\Gamma_{\mathrm{proof}}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{code}},\ \ell,\ h:\mathcal R(\ell,k)$.
The tail $\ell$ occurs in the specification but must not be available as a
program component. Erase it from the proof context and the induction hypothesis
is gone; expose it to the synthesiser and the candidate can bypass the intended
fold by calling a reference function on the tail. So context projection operates
on the \emph{executable} inventory and closes under type dependencies: if a
retained variable's type mentions another local, that dependency is retained or
explicitly abstracted. The full source context stays available to the checker.

Restricting the executable context is a completeness-affecting choice, and the
document treats it as one. The small interface is the \emph{preferred} search
branch; a budgeted branch with the original context stays queued. For a purely
first-order arithmetic hole, the constructive projection worker above can
replace enumeration outright, and a syntax-guided synthesis interface is an
available proposer for the rest~\cite{cvc5-sygus} --- as a proposer, whose
output is checked like any other.

\subsubsection*{Keep compatibility constraints; do not multiply projections}

Holes are not independent in general. Residual constraints should be stored as
finite or symbolic relations indexed by \emph{shared hole identifiers}. For
finite relations use natural joins: if $R_1$ mentions holes $(u,v)$ and $R_2$
mentions $(v,w)$, index both by the full identity of $v$, join matching rows,
and retain witnesses for every constraint. For symbolic relations, keep shared
metavariables owned by the same synthesis region and discharge the conjoined
residual before accepting an assembled term.

The indexing fragment happens to factor once the constructor contract is fixed
--- base, negative, zero and positive results have separate conditions --- and
its one-survivor-per-hole outcome must not be generalised into a claim that all
higher-order synthesis decomposes into a product.

The search object is an \emph{algebra region}: the exact fold occurrence, the
selected source polytype, the instantiated carrier, source universes, available
lexical binders, local instance evidence, the hole dependency relation, and the
residual contract. Every field is a reference into the existing typed-source
inventory, not a fresh spelling that will later have to be guessed back into the
original context. Search continuations are memoised by algebra region and hole,
not by pretty-printed type; a useful memo entry holds the next candidate cursor,
the local terms already considered, the remaining shared budget, and the
residual compatibility relation. Sibling holes may reuse a function head even
when an ancestor-path rule forbids repeating it along one application path ---
a distinction Djex's construction policies already make. None of this resets the
existing limits: local checks, joins, whole-term construction and verification
must all be charged. No SMT solver is required for a first version.

\begin{lstlisting}
completeFold(demand, specification):
  for spine in compatibleEliminationSpines(demand):
    R       := carrierEquation(spine)
    sketch  := instantiateFoldAt(R)
    contracts := deriveBaseAndStepContracts(specification, sketch)
    for tuple in join(localSearch(contracts)):
      term := assemble(sketch, tuple)
      if sourceTypeCheck(term) and proveAlgebraContracts(term):
        return applyCheckedFoldTheorem(term)
  return Unknown
\end{lstlisting}

The implemented fragment has one fold schema, one carrier spine, and a fixed
three-way integer case split. Automatic discovery of arbitrary recursive
schemas, branch predicates and source-level contracts is proposed, not built.

\subsubsection*{What the measured family does and does not show}

A controlled experiment compares two policies over the \emph{same} four finite
hole grammars in the \emph{same} within-hole order: a flat policy that
enumerates tuples and type-checks whole terms, and a local policy that first
filters each hole against its own contract, using distinct symbolic-looking
values for element binders and a continuation observation $k(j)=(\mathtt{tail},j)$.
With $n$ distractors the grammar sizes are $n+1$, $n+2$, $n+2$, $n+8$, and the
valid choices are placed deliberately late, so the successful flat ordinal is
\[
 (n+1)(n+2)^2(n+8)-(n+8),
\]
which is $50\,940$ at $n=12$, against $4n+13=61$ local component checks. Both
policies return the same term. The contrast is quartic against linear
\emph{in this deliberately unfavourable family}: fixed sketch, independent exact
local contracts, finite grammars, distinct distractors, and that stated order.
It is not a measured $50\,940$-fold speedup, not a complexity theorem for
unrestricted synthesis, and not evidence that the original Leant query has been
fixed.

\subsubsection*{The Church-encoding boundary, stated three times}

The diagnostic that motivates all of this uses a Church-encoded list, not Lean's
inductive \code{List}. With
$\operatorname{encode}(xs)(R,a,b)=\operatorname{foldr}(a,b,xs)$, a theorem about
$\operatorname{encode}(xs)$ is exactly that: a theorem about encoded finite
lists. It does not establish that every Lean value of a Church-style type
behaves as the encoding of a finite list. Transferring the sketch requires an
explicit representation law
\[
 \operatorname{Rep}(c,\ell)\Longrightarrow
 c\ B\ \operatorname{step}\ b_0=\operatorname{foldr}(\operatorname{step},b_0,\ell)
\]
at the exact carrier and universe selection in use. For an explicit encoding of
an ordinary list this is a routine fold law; for an arbitrary polymorphic value
it must not be inferred from the displayed type. Parametricity,
representability, totality and the available eliminations are substantive
assumptions, and they are especially substantive across a Haskell/Lean
boundary: the source \code{Int} is not automatically Lean's unbounded
\code{Int}, and the actual branch primitive, binder order, polymorphic
selections and dictionary payloads all have to be retained.

Three of the extension proposals reach this conclusion independently, in
different words and from different starting points, and none of them claims the
original query is solved. Two hand-written Lean specimens exist and are marked
uncompiled. A source type is not an automatic relational-parametricity theorem.

\subsubsection*{What to measure before claiming anything here}

Record the number of schema alternatives, candidate holes, full contract checks,
reusable hole theorems, and reconstructed whole programs. Compare on the same
outer query, source inventory, budgets and acceptance predicate. An isolated
hole success is diagnostic evidence, not a solved benchmark; keep a negative
control that actually reaches predicate checking rather than calling a
zero-candidate timeout a successful rejection. The composition hypothesis is
plausible from the inspected diagnostic and remains unmeasured on the real
search --- which is a reason to test it before changing any global search
penalty, not a reason to announce a Church-encoding milestone.

\subsection{Recursive obligations: cyclic candidates, checked descent}
\label{sec:cycles}

Tabling in Section~\ref{sec:architecture} forbids closing an active goal with
itself, and that rule stays. What the extension round changes is what happens
\emph{after} a recursive pattern has been found: the cyclic dependency may be
recorded as an unaccepted proof-program candidate whose recursive edges must
then be justified by well-founded descent. There is no need to trust an
arbitrary cyclic proof graph, and no need to implement a general cyclic-proof
kernel.

\subsubsection*{The rank lives on the tagged state}

Consider $P(n)\to Q(n)$ and $Q(n)\to P(n-1)$ for $n\ge1$. The first edge does
not decrease $n$ at all, and rejecting it in isolation would discard a perfectly
good mutual induction. Ranking the \emph{tagged} state resolves it:
\[
 \rho(P,n)=2n+1,\qquad \rho(Q,n)=2n,
\]
and now every edge drops by exactly one. The phase is not a tie-break in search
order; it is part of the termination measure. An acyclic run of zero-progress
edges suggests phases cheaply --- assign reverse topological heights in a
candidate phase graph, then scale the primary measure enough to dominate phase
resets --- but a cycle consisting purely of zero-progress edges cannot be
repaired by phases at all.

Two more shapes matter. For $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x-1)$ under $x\ge1$, neither ``the
first argument always decreases'' nor ``both arguments shrink'' is the right
test; $x+y$ drops by one, which also covers the subtractive Euclidean branches
when their positive-subtraction guards are kept. And where a lexicographic
measure is needed, the compiler must use lexicographic well-foundedness rather
than encoding $(x,y)$ as $ax+by$ with a guessed multiplier: such an encoding is
valid only when a bound on the lower-priority component is available \emph{and
proved}.

\subsubsection*{Size-change closure is a filter, not a proof}

Annotate each argument comparison with $0$ (unknown), $1$ (non-increase) or $2$
(strict decrease). Along a composable path an unknown annihilates that path;
otherwise the result is strict if either constituent is. Writing $\otimes$ for
path composition and $\oplus$ for maximum strength,
\[
 (G\circ H)_{ij}=\bigoplus_k G_{ik}\otimes H_{kj},
\]
compute the closure over \emph{non-empty} paths, keeping source and target node
tags. The standard size-change test asks for a strict diagonal entry in every
idempotent self-graph of that closure.

In this design the closure is diagnostic. It guides the ranking search and it is
never handed to Lean as a termination proof; the first compiler accepts only an
explicit checked ranking. A failed test means this abstraction did not certify
descent, and a passing test relies on every recorded local comparison being
semantically valid.

\subsubsection*{Synthesising the rank, and not trusting the solver that found it}

For a single affine rank $\rho_v(x)=b_v+\sum_i w_{vi}x_i$, introduce nonnegative
Farkas multipliers $\lambda_{ej}$ per edge and equate coefficients in
\[
 \rho_v(x)-\rho_w(F_e(x))-1=\sum_j\lambda_{ej}g_{ej}(x),
\]
where $g_{ej}$ are the edge's guards. Update and guard coefficients are fixed,
so this is linear feasibility in the rank parameters and multipliers. Solve over
$\Q$, scale to integral coefficients if needed, then regenerate and check every
exact identity: the solver's feasibility verdict is not the certificate. For a
lexicographic rank, choose a pivot per edge and enforce prefix equality; pivots
can be enumerated for small components or selected by an SMT/MILP proposer, but
the backend must export the chosen pivots and exact coefficient witnesses rather
than a rounded floating-point tuple.

The delivered prototype does bounded enumeration instead --- common weights
across nodes, small individual phases, then permutations of the original
coordinates as a lexicographic fallback, with cone membership solved by
enumerating linearly independent supports of size at most the affine coefficient
dimension. That suits its one- and two-variable fixtures and is not proposed as
a scalable substitute for an exact LP backend. All its coefficients and phases
are nonnegative integers, so every rank maps natural states to naturals; signed
coefficients are more expressive but then carry an additional obligation, namely
nonnegativity over each node's invariant region. They must not be admitted just
because they decrease along the sampled calls.

\subsubsection*{Lowering: ordinary well-founded recursion, no new proof rule}

A component is closed as follows. Package its node goals into a tagged family
$P$. Build a well-founded relation from the accepted rank. Construct one local
proof constructor per node from ordinary case splits and already-checked
non-recursive facts. Recursive edges become accesses to the well-founded
induction hypothesis, instantiated at the typed callee state and supplied with
the corresponding descent proof. Project the theorem for the entry node and the
original arguments.

\begin{invariant}[No self-authorised node]\label{inv:scc}
No node theorem is inserted into the environment as an assumed theorem while its
strongly connected component is being checked. The only recursive authority is
the induction hypothesis supplied by the well-founded recursor.
\end{invariant}

Lean already supports structural and well-founded recursive definitions through
ordinary kernel-checked terms; \code{termination\_by} and \code{decreasing\_by}
are possible elaboration interfaces, not a new trusted rule. Stage the work:
explicit tagged states and \emph{given} local constructors first, then graph
extraction. Discovering dependent back-edge substitutions, generating branches,
inferring ranks and lowering proofs all at once makes failures impossible to
localise.

\subsubsection*{An edge carries a telescope, not an argument list}

A cyclic edge must map the callee's entire dependent telescope. If a node
contains $n:\N$, $xs:\operatorname{Vector}(A,n)$ and a hypothesis about $xs$,
an edge that changes $n$ cannot keep the old vector or the old hypothesis by
identity: it must supply a vector at the new index and transport or reconstruct
the dependent premise. Equally, a proof established under a hypothesis $H(n_0)$
about one fixed current value cannot be silently generalised to $H(n)$ for all
$n$. Printed variable names do not identify a telescope, and an
alpha-normalised goal body alone does not determine which hypotheses may travel
to a recursive call.

\subsubsection*{Where this compiler is the wrong shape}

Two later efforts found the same limitation from opposite directions, and it is
a limitation of the \emph{criterion}, not of the search.

A winning strategy for a recurrence condition --- visit the good set infinitely
often --- produces plays that are correct \emph{and} infinite. No globally
strictly decreasing measure exists along such a play, because there cannot be
one. Insisting on well-founded descent everywhere therefore rejects a correct
proof, and no amount of additional ranking grammar repairs that. The fix is a
rank that decreases locally and is permitted to \emph{reset} on the good set,
which is what Section~\ref{sec:quantitative}'s parity and B\"uchi certificates
carry \src{r8, r9}.

The probabilistic case fails for a different reason with the same conclusion. A
loop can terminate with probability one while individual executions increase
any given measure; demanding a pointwise decrease demands something false. The
correct local obligation is a decrease \emph{in expectation}, and it must be
routed to the polynomial machinery of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear} in that form
rather than as the stronger pointwise condition \src{r8}.

Neither observation invalidates what this subsection builds. Well-founded
descent on a tagged state remains the right compilation for a terminating
recursive obligation, which is what it was introduced for. What both efforts
establish is that the obligation graph must be able to \emph{route away} from
it, and that a failure to find a global rank is not evidence that the recursion
is unsound.

\subsubsection*{The recorded outcomes}

On six supplied call graphs the prototype finds four rankings, all of which pass
an exact independent descent replay: $2n+1$/$2n$ for the two-phase graph after
eight attempts, $x+y$ for swapped arguments and for subtractive Euclid after
four each, and a lexicographic $(x,y)$ after sixteen. The identity cycle and an
alternating-decrease cycle return \unknown{}, with the size-change criterion
failing. Those two fixtures contain explicit bad cycles for independent reasons;
in general a failed size-change test or an exhausted ranking grammar remains
inconclusive. These are call-graph results. They are not completed Lean proofs
of recursive theorems.
